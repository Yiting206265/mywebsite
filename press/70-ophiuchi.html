Suns set on hunt for giant planets in nearby binary system

A century of observations closes one chapter in the 171-year search for planets around 70 Ophiuchi AB—and helps chart the next one.

70 Ophiuchi AB · 16 light-years away

New research led by the University of Michigan represents the most sensitive modern search for giant planets in 70 Ophiuchi AB, one of the nearest binary star systems to Earth.

A planetary claim from 1855

British astronomer William Jacob proposed in 1855 that an unseen planet might explain irregularities in the motion of the system’s two stars. The claim did not survive later scrutiny, but increasingly precise measurements still left open the possibility that Jupiter-sized planets could orbit within the system’s inner regions.

A century of data

Our team combined archival observations spanning roughly a century with new precision radial-velocity measurements from the Magellan Clay Telescope in Chile and the Harlan J. Smith Telescope in Texas. The analysis showed that an apparent wobble in the stars comes from changes on their surfaces rather than the pull of a giant planet.

The results rule out planets at least as massive as Jupiter within 5 astronomical units of 70 Oph A and within half an astronomical unit of 70 Oph B. The two stars’ 88.38-year orbit is now also among the best characterized binary orbits in the sky.

“It’s a mix of disappointment and excitement. We’re finding out that there isn’t a Jupiter-sized planet, but we’re also opening the latest chapter on this historic system.”

The door remains open

The result does not rule out every planet. Saturn-sized and smaller worlds remain below the sensitivity of this search, and simulations show that an Earth-mass planet could maintain a stable orbit in the habitable zone around the primary star. No such planet has been detected, but the new limits tell future observatories where—and how—to look.

That makes 70 Ophiuchi AB a compelling target for future missions studying how planets form and survive in systems with more than one sun.
